# Narrative scaffolds — pick one, apply it consistently

A scaffold is a starting structure, not a cage. Audience-specific sequences built on these (executive,
investor, students, technical, self-read, demo) are in deck-playbooks.md; exhibit choice per slide is in
charts-and-diagrams.md. Choose from audience + goal; deviate on purpose, not
by accident. Each entry: when to use, the ordered beats, the failure mode.

---

## The Case  — recommend a decision to people with authority
**Use:** leadership/board buy-in on a recommendation.
**Beats:** Situation → Tension (the gap/risk) → Options (2–3 real ones, brief) → Recommendation (state
it plainly) → Evidence (2–3 slides, one point each) → Risk + mitigation (own it) → Cost of inaction
(make the status quo uncomfortable) → The Ask (precise) → Next steps.
**Fails when:** you present four options while clearly believing one (just recommend it); you skip the
cost of inaction; the ask is vague.

## The Pitch — ask for money / partnership / resources
**Beats:** Problem (visceral, quantified) → Why now (what changed) → Solution (one sentence, then show
it) → How it works (just enough to be credible) → Traction/proof → Market → Business model → Team
(unfair advantages, not résumés) → The Ask ("$X to reach Y by Z") → Close (one line).
**Fails when:** the ask is buried on slide 15; you lead with the team instead of the problem.

## The Keynote — shift how an audience thinks
**Beats:** Opening scene (concrete, 15s, a story not a stat) → Conventional wisdom (state it fairly;
they nod) → The crack (the anomaly that breaks it) → The reframe (your thesis, one sentence — the slide
they remember) → 3 evidence beats (vary the *type* of evidence) → Implication (so what changes) →
The charge (what to do/believe, with energy).
**Fails when:** you open with "thanks for having me"; more than three beats; you close with "Questions?"

## The Workshop — teach a skill or framework
**Use:** training, enablement, a method the audience will apply.
**Beats:** Why this matters (stakes) → Teach the method once (the spine) → Apply it (example 1) →
Apply it again (example 2, varied) → … → Synthesize the pattern across examples → Practice/next step.
Teaching the framework once and then running it repeatedly is what makes it stick (and lets the
audience predict what comes next). Great for multi-module decks (→ zoom navigation).

## The Report-out — update stakeholders who know the context
**Beats:** Headline (the one thing, first) → Scorecard (metrics vs plan, 5-second scan) → What worked
(specific: action→result) → What didn't (same specificity; pair each miss with a diagnosis) → The
insight (one non-obvious learning) → Next-period plan (2–3 priorities tied to metrics) → Ask.
**Fails when:** 20 slides on what happened, one on what it means; misses hidden.

## Answer-first (BLUF) — senior, time-pressured
Lead with the conclusion, then support it. For grant panels, execs, short slots where the audience may
not stay for the full build-up. State the answer on slide 2–3, then: evidence, then implications.

## SCR / SCQA — analytical / research claim  (Minto Pyramid)
**Situation** (what's established) → **Complication** (what's missing/broken/contested) →
[**Question** it raises] → **Resolution/Answer** (your contribution). The Pyramid Principle: lead with
the governing thought, then group supporting arguments beneath it, each itself a mini-pyramid. Strong
default for technical peers and papers. *Source: Barbara Minto, McKinsey.*

## Monroe's Motivated Sequence — persuade to act (emotional)
**Attention → Need (make them feel the problem) → Satisfaction (your solution) → Visualization (paint
the after) → Action (the specific ask).** Where answer-first leads with the answer, Monroe spends time
making the audience *feel* the need first. *Source: Alan Monroe, Purdue, 1930s.*

## Duarte Sparkline — a moving talk
Oscillate between **"what is"** and **"what could be"**, building tension and release in waves, and end
on a **"new bliss."** Not a linear list of facts; a rhythm. *Source: Nancy Duarte, "Resonate."*

## Assertion–Evidence — slide-level structure (pairs with any scaffold)
Every content slide = a **declarative assertion** as the headline (a full sentence, the takeaway) +
**visual evidence** that supports it (chart/image/diagram), not a bullet list. This is exactly the
"action title + one exhibit" rule the engineer enforces per slide. *Source: Michael Alley, Penn State.*

## Structural pieces worth stealing (from Figma's "60 ideas" and NIU's tutorial)

**Organisation patterns for a section's points** (NIU) — pick the one that fits the content:
spatial · chronological · causal · topical · problem→solution.

**Beginning / Body / End** (NIU): plan the **body first**; the opening and close fall into place after.
Opening = grab attention + stay relevant (a relevant statistic, a quote, or a question — *write it
after the body exists*). End = restate the purpose, summarise the major points, close on something
memorable. Never trail from the body straight into Q&A with no real conclusion.

**Agenda / navigation:** a table-of-contents / map slide up front previews structure and doubles as the
zoom hub for multi-section decks (Figma idea 27). A timeline slide shows sequence and milestones.

**Openers that work** (Figma): a thought-provoking question; a powerful (context-checked) quote; a
striking statistic/fact that frames the problem.

**Closers that work:** a clear call to action (the ask) on the final slide; a one-line summary of
takeaways. The conclusions slide stays on screen during Q&A.

**The 5/5/5 guideline** (Figma): ≤5 words/line, ≤5 lines/slide, ≤5 text-heavy slides in a row — a
rough ceiling, breakable with intent. NIU's version: ≤5–7 bullets/slide, ≤2–3 lines/bullet, phrases
not sentences; headings ≥28pt, bullets ≥24pt, readable from the back row.

**Pacing:** vary cadence and volume to mark emphasis; set explicit time limits on Q&A.

**Sources:** Figma, *60 creative presentation ideas* (figma.com/resource-library/presentation-ideas);
NIU, *Effective Presentation Skills Tutorial* (niu.edu/presentations). Both are practitioner guidance,
not empirical studies — treat as craft, not law.
